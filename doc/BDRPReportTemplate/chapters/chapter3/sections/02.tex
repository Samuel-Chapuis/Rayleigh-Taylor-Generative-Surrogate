\section{Rayleigh--Taylor Instability}

\subsection{Physical Mechanism}

The Rayleigh--Taylor instability (RTI) is a hydrodynamic instability that occurs when a dense fluid is accelerated by a lighter one. Under such conditions, perturbations located at the interface between the two fluids may grow over time and generate complex non-linear flow structures.

This instability appears in many physical systems involving strong density gradients and acceleration effects, including astrophysical flows, supernova explosions, atmospheric phenomena, and inertial confinement fusion plasmas. In the context of high-energy-density physics, RTI plays a major role because it can strongly enhance turbulent mixing and degrade the stability of compressible flows.

In inertial confinement fusion configurations, the instability develops during the implosion phase when the dense shell material is accelerated inward by the pressure generated by the ablation of the outer surface. Small imperfections initially present on the capsule surface are amplified during compression and progressively distort the interface. The idealized mechanism is represented in Figure~\ref{fig:rti_principle}.

As the instability evolves, the interface develops characteristic structures commonly referred to as \textit{bubbles} and \textit{spikes}. The lighter fluid penetrates the denser region through rising bubbles, while the denser fluid penetrates downward through spikes. At later stages, non-linear interactions between structures lead to the formation of increasingly complex flow patterns and eventually to turbulent mixing.

The growth of RTI strongly depends on several physical parameters, including the density contrast between the two fluids, the acceleration field, the viscosity, compressibility effects, and the initial perturbation spectrum. One important dimensionless quantity used to characterize the instability is the Atwood number~\citep[p.~22]{zhou2017rti1}, defined as

\begin{equation}
    A = \frac{\rho_h - \rho_l}{\rho_h + \rho_l}
\end{equation}

where $\rho_h$ and $\rho_l$ denote the densities of the heavy and light fluids respectively. Large Atwood numbers generally correspond to stronger instability growth.


\subsection{Mathematical Origin of the Rayleigh--Taylor Instability}

The Rayleigh--Taylor instability naturally arises from the governing equations of fluid motion when a density stratification is subjected to an acceleration directed from the heavier fluid toward the lighter one. In the classical formulation of Chandrasekhar~\cite{chandrasekhar1981hydrodynamic}, the instability is most directly described by following the displacement of the material interface separating two fluids, rather than by first decomposing all flow variables into a base state and volumetric perturbations.

Let $\rho$ denote the fluid density, $\mathbf{u}$ the velocity field, $p$ the pressure, $\mu$ the dynamic viscosity, $\mathbf{g}$ the imposed acceleration, and $t_{\mathrm{phys}}$ the physical time. For an incompressible Newtonian fluid, the conservation equations read

\begin{align}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u} &= 0,\\
\rho\left(
\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t_{\mathrm{phys}}}
+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}
\right)
&=
-\nabla p
+\mu\nabla^2\mathbf{u}
+\rho\mathbf{g},
\end{align}

In the absence of motion, the velocity vanishes and the pressure reduces to a hydrostatic reference pressure $p_0$. The corresponding equilibrium satisfies

\begin{equation}
\nabla p_0=\rho\mathbf{g}.
\end{equation}

Consider two inviscid incompressible fluids separated, at equilibrium, by the plane $z=0$. The coordinate $z$ is taken opposite to the imposed acceleration. Let $g>0$ denote the magnitude of this acceleration and let $\mathbf{e}_z$ denote the unit vector in the positive $z$ direction, so that $\mathbf{g}=-g\mathbf{e}_z$. The coordinates parallel to the unperturbed interface are denoted by $\mathbf{x}_{\parallel}=(x,y)$. The perturbed material interface is represented by its vertical displacement $\eta(\mathbf{x}_{\parallel},t_{\mathrm{phys}})$ through the graph

\begin{equation}
z=\eta(\mathbf{x}_{\parallel},t_{\mathrm{phys}}),
\end{equation}

where $\eta$ is assumed small compared with the perturbation wavelength. In the Rayleigh--Taylor configuration considered here, the upper fluid is the heavy fluid, with density $\rho_h$, and occupies $z>\eta$, while the lower fluid is the light fluid, with density $\rho_l$, and occupies $z<\eta$. Thus $\rho_h>\rho_l$: the heavier fluid lies in the direction opposite to the acceleration and is accelerated toward the lighter one.

In the linear inviscid limit, the motion induced in each fluid can be represented by a velocity potential $\phi_j$, where the index $j$ is equal to $h$ in the heavy fluid and $l$ in the light fluid. The corresponding velocity field in fluid $j$ is denoted by $\mathbf{u}_j$. Since each fluid is incompressible and irrotational at this order, $\phi_j$ satisfies Laplace's equation:

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{u}_j=\nabla \phi_j,
\qquad
\nabla^2\phi_j=0,
\qquad
j\in\{h,l\}.
\end{equation}

Let $\mathbf{k}$ be the horizontal wave vector of an interfacial disturbance, $k=|\mathbf{k}|$ its wavenumber, $\gamma$ its temporal growth rate, $\hat{\eta}$ its complex amplitude, and $i$ the imaginary unit. A normal mode of the interface is written

\begin{equation}
\eta(\mathbf{x}_{\parallel},t_{\mathrm{phys}})
=
\hat{\eta}
\exp\left(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\parallel}+\gamma t_{\mathrm{phys}}\right),
\end{equation}

Let $\hat{\phi}_l$ and $\hat{\phi}_h$ denote the complex amplitudes of the velocity potentials in the light and heavy fluids. Since the perturbation must decay away from the interface, the potentials take the form

\begin{align}
\phi_l &=
\hat{\phi}_l
e^{kz}
\exp\left(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\parallel}+\gamma t_{\mathrm{phys}}\right),
\qquad z<0,\\
\phi_h &=
\hat{\phi}_h
e^{-kz}
\exp\left(i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{\parallel}+\gamma t_{\mathrm{phys}}\right),
\qquad z>0.
\end{align}

The interface is material: fluid particles initially on the interface remain on it. Linearizing this kinematic condition at the unperturbed interface $z=0$ gives, for each fluid $j\in\{h,l\}$,

\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \eta}{\partial t_{\mathrm{phys}}}
=
\left.\frac{\partial \phi_j}{\partial z}\right|_{z=0},
\end{equation}

Hence

\begin{equation}
\hat{\phi}_l=\frac{\gamma}{k}\hat{\eta},
\qquad
\hat{\phi}_h=-\frac{\gamma}{k}\hat{\eta}.
\end{equation}

The stability criterion follows from the dynamic condition at the same interface. In the absence of surface tension, the pressure is continuous across $z=\eta$. Combining this pressure continuity with the linearized unsteady Bernoulli relation in each fluid gives

\begin{equation}
\rho_h\frac{\partial\phi_h}{\partial t_{\mathrm{phys}}}
-
\rho_l\frac{\partial\phi_l}{\partial t_{\mathrm{phys}}}
+
g(\rho_h-\rho_l)\eta
=0
\qquad \text{at } z=0.
\end{equation}

Substituting the kinematic relations into this interfacial balance yields the classical Rayleigh--Taylor dispersion relation

\begin{equation}
\gamma^2
=
gk
\frac{\rho_h-\rho_l}{\rho_h+\rho_l}
=
A g k.
\end{equation}

The density ratio is exactly the Atwood number defined above. Since $\rho_h>\rho_l$ in the unstable ordering, $\gamma^2>0$. For an initial interface amplitude $\eta_0$, the displacement grows exponentially as

\begin{equation}
\eta(t_{\mathrm{phys}})=\eta_0e^{\gamma t_{\mathrm{phys}}},
\end{equation}

so the hydrostatic equilibrium is unstable. If the density ordering were reversed, the numerator in the dispersion relation would change sign and the same interface mode would oscillate rather than grow. This interfacial balance is the mathematical origin of the Rayleigh--Taylor instability and explains why small displacements evolve into the nonlinear bubbles and spikes observed experimentally and numerically.




\subsection{Linear and Non-linear Regimes}

During the early stages of the instability, perturbations remain sufficiently small for linear stability theory to apply. In the incompressible inviscid limit, the perturbation amplitude grows exponentially with growth rate~\citep[p.~23]{zhou2017rti1}

\[
\gamma = \sqrt{A g k},
\]

where $A$ is the Atwood number, $g$ the acceleration, and $k$ the perturbation wavenumber.

As the perturbation amplitude increases, non-linear effects progressively become dominant. Mode coupling mechanisms appear and coherent structures interact across multiple spatial scales. The flow then transitions toward a turbulent mixing regime characterized by strong intermittency and multi-scale dynamics.

\subsection{Multi-scale Nature and Turbulence}

The multi-scale character of a flow should not be understood as a purely
numerical effect or as a simple consequence of looking at the same field with
different grid resolutions. It is first a physical property of flows in which
inertia, advection, viscosity, and coherent structures interact over a range of
length scales. A classical experimental illustration is the flow past a sphere
at increasing Reynolds number
$Re=\rho U D/\mu$, where $\rho$ is the fluid density, $U$ the incoming velocity,
$D$ the sphere diameter, and $\mu$ the dynamic viscosity. This evolution is
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:reynolds_sphere_wakes} from the visualizations
collected by Van Dyke~\citep{vandDyke1982album}. For low Reynolds number, the
streamlines remain mostly organized and the wake is limited. As the Reynolds
number increases, the separated region grows, vortical structures appear in the
wake, and the flow progressively develops smaller spatial features embedded in
larger coherent motions.

The Rayleigh--Taylor instability follows the same general mechanism of
progressive scale enrichment, although the physical source of the motion is
different. Instead of a mean flow separating behind an obstacle, RTI starts from
an accelerated density interface. The early dynamics are dominated by the
growth of large interface perturbations, while the non-linear regime generates
bubbles, spikes, shear layers, and secondary vortices. As these structures
interact, the mixing layer develops fine-scale gradients and intermittent
features superimposed on the large coherent organization of the flow.

This analogy is useful for the present work because the density fields generated
by RTI simulations also contain coherent structures, intermittent mixing
regions, and localized gradients over several spatial scales. Capturing this
joint presence of large-scale organization and small-scale details motivates the
use of multi-resolution analysis tools capable of retaining both spatial
localization and scale-dependent information. This aspect will later motivate
the investigation of wavelet-based representations for generative modeling
purposes.
