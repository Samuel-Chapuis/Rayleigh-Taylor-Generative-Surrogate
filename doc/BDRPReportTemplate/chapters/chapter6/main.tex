
﻿\chapter{Conclusion and Perspectives}
\label{chap:conclusion}

The objective of this work was to investigate diffusion-based generative
surrogate models for two-dimensional Rayleigh--Taylor instability fields
obtained from Direct Numerical Simulations. More specifically, the study aimed
to confront two strategies: a classical diffusion model trained directly in
physical space, and wavelet-based diffusion models designed to exploit the
multi-scale structure of the instability. This comparison was motivated by the
high computational cost of DNS, the limited size of the available dataset, and
the strongly hierarchical nature of RTI flows.

The first part of the report introduced the physical and numerical framework of
the problem. Rayleigh--Taylor instability was presented as a non-linear and
multi-scale hydrodynamic instability characterized by the growth of bubbles,
spikes, sharp density gradients, and turbulent mixing structures. These
features make DNS particularly valuable, but also expensive. As a consequence,
the resulting datasets remain relatively small compared with those usually used
to train modern generative models. This limitation motivated the search for
representations and learning strategies adapted to structured physical fields.

Fourier and wavelet decompositions were then discussed as possible ways to
analyze and reduce the dimensional complexity of RTI density fields. Fourier
representations are natural for periodic flows and spectral analysis, but their
global basis functions are less adapted to localized gradients and intermittent
structures. Wavelet decompositions, on the other hand, provide a
multi-resolution representation localized both in space and scale. This makes
them attractive for RTI fields, where large coherent structures coexist with
localized small-scale fluctuations.

A first diffusion pipeline was implemented directly in image space. The model
was trained on preprocessed density fields using physically consistent data
augmentations, namely horizontal cyclic translations and horizontal
reflections. The generated samples showed that the baseline SGM was able to
learn a non-trivial approximation of the RTI data distribution. Qualitatively,
the generated fields recovered the main vertical organization of the mixing
zone and reproduced bubble-and-spike-like structures. Quantitatively, the
generated dataset remained much closer to the validation set than pure noise or
unrelated images. In particular, the PhyFID score between validation and
generated samples was approximately $0.132$, compared with $6.04$ for pure
noise. The physical metrics based on line-wise density profiles and fluctuation
spectra confirmed the same trend.

The second pipeline introduced wavelet coefficients into the generative
process. In this formulation, the density field is decomposed into a coarse
approximation and detail coefficients, and the diffusion model is trained to
generate fine-scale information conditioned on the large-scale structure. This
approach introduces an explicit multi-resolution inductive bias and provides a
more interpretable separation between the different spatial scales of the
problem. The obtained results show that the model can learn relationships
between approximation and detail coefficients, and that the wavelet framework
offers a meaningful way to analyze where reconstruction errors occur.

However, the current wavelet-based diffusion model does not yet outperform the
classical image-space baseline. The reconstructed fields remain rougher and
contain more visible artifacts, especially in the generated detail
coefficients. This result is important because it shows that introducing a
physically motivated representation is not sufficient by itself: the diffusion
architecture, the conditioning mechanism, the normalization of coefficient
channels, and the choice of wavelet level must all be carefully adapted to the
statistical structure of the transformed data. At the present stage, the direct
diffusion model therefore provides the best generation quality, while the
wavelet approach remains a promising but incomplete direction.

Several perspectives naturally follow from this work. First, the wavelet-based
pipeline should be evaluated with the same quantitative metrics as the
baseline, including PhyFID, line-wise density statistics, and fluctuation
spectra. This would make it possible to determine precisely which physical
properties are degraded or preserved by the wavelet formulation. Second,
alternative wavelet families and decomposition levels should be investigated,
since they directly control the separation between coarse and fine scales.
Third, the conditioning strategy could be improved, for example by using
multi-level conditioning or by generating the wavelet scales progressively from
coarse to fine. Finally, larger and more diverse DNS datasets would help assess
the generalization capabilities of both approaches and reduce the risk of
overfitting.

Overall, this work demonstrates that diffusion models are a relevant framework
for learning generative surrogate models of Rayleigh--Taylor instability
fields. The classical SGM baseline already captures meaningful statistical and
physical information from a limited DNS dataset. The wavelet-based formulation,
although not yet competitive in its current implementation, opens an
interesting path toward more interpretable and scale-aware generative models
for turbulent physical systems.
