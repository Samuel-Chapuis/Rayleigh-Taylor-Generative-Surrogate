
﻿\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF) is a major field of high-energy-density physics, in which matter is subjected to extreme pressures and temperatures over very short time scales. The objective is to compress and heat a small quantity of fusion fuel sufficiently to initiate thermonuclear reactions before the target disassembles. Understanding the hydrodynamic phenomena governing such implosions is therefore essential, as their efficiency depends on the ability to achieve a strong and nearly symmetric compression while limiting the growth of perturbations and material mixing.

In laser-driven ICF, a small capsule containing deuterium--tritium fuel is irradiated by high-intensity laser beams. The rapid heating and ablation of the outer layer generates a reaction force that accelerates the remaining shell inward, compressing the fuel toward the center of the target. During this process, the capsule experiences extreme thermodynamic conditions involving strong compression, shock-wave propagation, and complex multi-scale hydrodynamic phenomena. If the implosion is sufficiently symmetric and the compression sufficiently intense, the central region can reach the density and temperature required for fusion reactions to occur. The basic laser-driven implosion mechanism is summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:icf_principle}.

A major difficulty in ICF lies in maintaining the stability of the implosion. Small perturbations initially present at material interface may grow during the implosion, promoting hydrodynamic mixing and progressively degrading the thermodynamic conditions within the central hot spot. Among the hydrodynamic instabilities involved in this process, the Rayleigh--Taylor instability (RTI) plays a central role. It occurs when a dense fluid is accelerated by a lighter one and leads to the development of characteristic bubble and spike structures. In the context of ICF, this instability may enhance turbulent mixing between the shell material and the fuel, thereby reducing the achievable temperature and compromising ignition conditions.

Predicting the evolution of the Rayleigh--Taylor instability remains challenging because of its non-linear, multi-scale, and eventually turbulent nature. Direct Numerical Simulations (DNS) provide a reference approach to study such flows, as they resolve the relevant spatial and temporal scales without relying on turbulence closure models \citep{MoinMahesh1998}. Therefore they make it possible to generate accurate representations of the instability dynamics, from the early growth phase to more complex mixing regimes, including the bubble, spike, and mixing structures documented in the RTI literature \citep{KULL1991197,zhou2017rti1}. The physical mechanism of RTI, the numerical role of DNS, and the mathematical representations used to analyze the resulting density fields are developed in Chapter~\ref{chap:background}. Figure~\ref{fig:dns_example} shows an example of the type of density fields considered in this work.

However, DNS remain computationally expensive: their cost grows with spatial resolution, simulated duration, and model complexity, so only limited training ensembles can usually be produced. This is a critical bottleneck for uncertainty quantification, sensitivity analysis, parameter scans, optimization, or statistical forecasting, where thousands or millions of realizations may be needed. The objective is therefore not only to reproduce one trajectory, but to build a surrogate able to generate large ensembles of plausible RTI density fields at a fraction of the DNS cost. This motivation is consistent with reduced-order, multifidelity, and machine-learning approaches in computational fluid mechanics, while retaining the usual concerns about data requirements, physical consistency, and extrapolation \citep{Peherstorfer2018,Brunton2020MLFluid}. For such a surrogate, sampling throughput is part of the scientific requirement.

Generative models provide a natural formulation: they approximate the distribution represented by the DNS dataset and can sample new realizations from it. Score-based diffusion models are attractive because they can represent complex high-dimensional distributions without imposing a low-dimensional parametric form \citep{Song2021SDE}. Their sampling cost is nevertheless significant, since generation requires integrating a reverse-time stochastic differential equation, or its probability-flow counterpart, with one score-network evaluation per step \citep{ANDERSON1982313}.

This motivates the wavelet-based strategy introduced by Guth, Coste, De Bortoli, and Mallat in the Wavelet Score-Based Generative Model (WSGM) \citep{Guth2022WSGM}. WSGM factorizes generation across scales: a coarse representation is sampled first, then wavelet details are generated conditionally from coarse to fine. For Gaussian multiscale distributions, the authors show that the discretization error can be controlled without increasing the number of time steps per image pixel with resolution; their experiments also report convergence with far fewer integration steps than a conventional SGM, in some cases by roughly two orders of magnitude. This is not a universal guarantee for arbitrary RTI fields, but it identifies a concrete route toward faster diffusion sampling.

The present study adapts this idea to two-dimensional Rayleigh--Taylor density fields. Wavelets are relevant here because they combine spatial and scale localization, a property useful for turbulent flows where coherent large-scale structures coexist with localized fine-scale fluctuations \citep{Farge1992,Mallat2009,Meyer1992}. Each field is decomposed into one approximation component, $LL$, and three directional detail components, $LH$, $HL$, and $HH$. In the implemented pipeline, an initial coarse approximation can be sampled by an unconditional diffusion model, after which a cascade of conditional diffusion models generates the details level by level. At each level, the current coarse field conditions the detail model; the sampled details are then combined with it through an inverse wavelet transform to produce the next finer approximation. Figure~\ref{fig:introduction_wavelet_diffusion_scheme} summarizes the high-level distinction between direct image-space diffusion and the wavelet-based formulation. The preprocessing choices, the direct image-space baseline, and this cascade are described in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

\textbf{The central hypothesis is that this scale-conditioned cascade, by separating large-scale organization from localized fine-scale fluctuations, can provide a more efficient and better-conditioned generative representation of RTI fields than a conventional image-space diffusion model.}

The study therefore asks whether a direct score-based baseline can learn the RTI distribution from a limited dataset, and whether the adapted WSGM formulation can improve sampling efficiency or statistical fidelity without degrading the physical relevance of the generated fields. The experimental protocol and comparison with DNS references are presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:experiments}; Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} discusses the limitations of the current wavelet formulation and the conditions required to scale the surrogate further.

\newpage

\textbf{The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Implementation of a baseline diffusion model trained directly on preprocessed density fields;
    \item Construction of physically consistent data augmentations adapted to the symmetries and periodicity of the dataset;
    \item Study of Fourier and wavelet representations for dimensionality reduction and multi-scale analysis;
    \item Adaptation of the WSGM coarse-to-fine principle to a conditional diffusion pipeline for RTI wavelet coefficients;
    \item Definition and use of evaluation criteria combining feature-space distances with physical indicators, including density profiles and scale-dependent fluctuation statistics.
\end{enumerate}
